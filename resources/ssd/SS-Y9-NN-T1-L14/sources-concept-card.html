Page 156–158 · Reading 1

How did the dromedary camel benefit societies in the past? How important is the city of Muweilah to the history of the UAE? Activity 1: Read “The Domestication of the Camel”. The book says that for about 10,000 years people in the UAE area shared the land with large herds of wild camels; at first they hunted them, using the meat for food and the hide for clothes. Researchers are not sure exactly when camels were first domesticated, but domesticated camels were present in the UAE by 1,000 BCE, and some of the earliest evidence in the world for domesticated camels is found in the UAE.

The book says that once the domesticated camel appeared, lives changed forever. Camels gave much greater quantities of milk than sheep or goats, including during long desert trips. Most important, camels allowed people to travel long distances through the desert: before this people lived in the interior oases like Al Ain, in the mountains, or on the coast like Umm an-Nar Island, and did not travel much. With the dromedary camel they could transport goods across the whole country, which created a more connected economy and led to towns and villages expanding in the deserts.

Page 158–159 · Reading 2

Questions: How did people first use camels? (a. They domesticated camels. b. They killed camels for their meat and hides. c. They got milk from camels.) By what date are researchers sure that domesticated camels existed in the UAE? How important was it that people domesticated the dromedary camel? How did the camel’s ability to travel through the desert affect the economy of the UAE area?

Activity 2: In groups, discuss. The falaj and the domesticated dromedary camel both were part of life that emerged in the UAE area around 1,000 BCE: how did they each make a large impact on the people of the area? Then choose a recent invention that you think has changed life for people today, explain why it is important and how it has changed people’s lives.

Page 160–162 · Reading 3

Activity 3: Read “Muweilah”. The book says that with the expansion of overland trade, new towns arose, one of them now known as Muweilah, in modern-day Sharjah near Sharjah Airport. Excavation revealed a large settlement from about 1,000 to 700 BCE. Muweilah grew rapidly as trade between the coast and the desert increased: at first people lived in campsites, later mudbrick buildings were built, and after 900 BCE people dug a massive ditch and built walls for protection. About 200 years later the town was attacked and burned; most people seem to have escaped but some died as the walls collapsed. Why it was attacked remains a mystery, and it was very wealthy when destroyed.

The book says the destruction preserved many artefacts, among them the lid of a large incense burner with a figurine of a bull, and other incense burners with figurines of camels, showing how important the camel was. The use of incense became increasingly common during the Iron Age and began a tradition that is still important in UAE culture. Muweilah is one of many desert towns that grew after the camel was domesticated; their people began to trade with Yemen and Oman for frankincense and other aromatics, and eventually camel-borne trade reached cities such as Palmyra in Syria, marking the beginning of a new stage in UAE history.

Questions: Where was Muweilah located? About what time was it an active city? Why did it grow rapidly? About when was it attacked? What was the artefact in the picture and what was it used for? Because of the domesticated camel, who did people of the UAE area trade with?

Page 163–169 · Reading 5

Activity 6: Read about Mleiha. The book says Mleiha, in inland Sharjah, rose to prominence about 0–300 BCE and became a crossroads for trade routes. Its remains cover at least 2.5 by 2 kilometres and its size and organisation were without parallel in the ancient UAE; the buildings were spread out and most were simple homes, probably of farmers. One of the most important discoveries is the use of coins, a first for the area, which the book says shows that Mleiha had significant power. Coins also give information about culture, leadership and symbolism, often showing the leader’s image and name and how groups interacted.

Activity 7: The book says Mleiha’s tombs resemble tombs in other areas of Arabia, and several inscriptions reveal contact with the rest of Arabia, one written in the South Arabian alphabet of what is now Yemen. It says this is the first example of writing by the people of the UAE area at that time; a three-letter inscription from Muweilah dates to around 800 BCE, but only after 300 BCE did South Arabian and Aramaic become more common, and even then writing was never widespread, used on tombs or decorated metal bowls. Most people did not read or write, but they shared stories and songs about their history and identity: songs of fishermen and pearl divers, poetry recited from the heart, and dances such as the “Ayala” practised today.

Activity 8: Writing first emerged in the UAE area during the Mleiha period. How important is it for a community to help its people learn to read and write? What should a government do, and what should families and individuals do? Discuss. Activity 9: All communities express identity through the arts. Describe an art form from another culture, find a picture or video of it, show it to other students, and describe what it symbolises.
