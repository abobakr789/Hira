The UAE landscape and Jebel Faya

The book explains that, according to scientists, early humans travelled to Arabia and left tools at Jebel Faya, that the climate turned dry and then wetter around 10,000 BCE, and that people of the Neolithic Age at Jebel Buhais hunted, kept herds and lived in a changing land.

Page 120–122 · Reading 1

How does climate change affect where people live? How did people in Jebel Buhais live in the Neolithic Age? How did people trade in the Neolithic Age in the Arabian Peninsula? Warm-up: In groups, discuss what makes life in the UAE easy and what makes it challenging. Think of the weather, housing, getting food, transportation and so on. Activity 1: In groups discuss: What do you think makes the climate and landscape of an area change? Were the changes in the climate and landscape in the past natural or caused by humans? How are humans making changes in the landscape and climate today?

Page 122–125 · Reading 2

Activity 2: Answer the questions about the movement of humans to the Arabian Peninsula. Mark the approximate location of Jebel Faya on the map. Why do scientists believe people travelled from Africa through Jebel Faya as well as Egypt? How was the climate different at that time compared to today? What was the landscape like in Jebel Faya? Why did people stop coming? What evidence is there that humans lived in Jebel Faya?

Activity 3: Read the text. The book says that, according to scientists, modern humans emerged around 200,000 years ago in Africa and then spread around the world. It says fossils found in caves at Qafzeh and Skhul suggest humans came from Africa through the Nile Valley into Palestine between 120,000 and 80,000 years ago. The book says excavations at Jebel Faya in what is now Sharjah show that humans also travelled across the Arabian Peninsula: archaeologists found stone tools, such as small hand-axes and tools for cutting up food, similar to tools found in East Africa, buried between about 130,000 and 95,000 years ago.

The book says that around 130,000 years ago the Red Sea was much lower than today, so crossing from Africa to Arabia would have been like crossing a small river, and that there was more rainfall, with grasslands and large numbers of wild animals that people could hunt. Questions: What continent did humans come from? What two ways did humans move out of Africa? What were the tools made from? What two purposes for the tools are mentioned? What was the landscape of Jebel Faya like 130,000 years ago?

Page 126–129 · Reading 3

Activity 4: Complete the table about life in Jebel Faya: food, fresh water, clothing and shelter, where a shelter is a place to live that is safe from the weather and enemies. Activity 5: Imagine that you lived in Jebel Faya in the past. What skills would you have to learn to meet your basic human needs? How would you learn them?

Activity 6: Read “Climate Change Starting in 10,000 BCE”. The book says from about 36,000 to 10,000 BCE a thick layer of sand built up at Jebel Faya, which indicates a very dry climate, and that few people probably lived in the UAE region then. In about 10,000 BCE the climate started changing again and the area received much more rain; scientists believe the Indian Ocean monsoons moved into Arabia, bringing cooler temperatures and more rainfall. Grasslands expanded into the interior and people moved from place to place with the seasons. They began to keep domesticated cattle, sheep and goats for meat, milk and wool, in herds moved around during the year. The book says keeping animals has always been an important part of life in the UAE.

Activity 7: Answer the questions. What happened to the climate in the times listed? What brought more rain to the Arabian Peninsula? Fill in the sequence of events: the Indian Ocean monsoons moved into Arabia, temperatures fell, more rain came, more people moved to the interior, and people kept animals in herds. How did the weather affect people’s activities?

Page 130–132 · Reading 4

Activity 8: Read the text “Jebel Buhais”. The book says people in the Arabian Peninsula hunted wild animals such as oryx, gazelle and camels, and developed stone tool technologies, chipping both faces of a stone blade to make fine arrowheads and cutting tools. The period from about 8000 BCE to 3000 BCE is called the “Neolithic Age” or “New Stone Age”, and the site of Jebel Buhais in Sharjah is important for understanding it.

The book says that by studying bones and teeth found at Jebel Buhais, scientists learned about health and diet: women lived on average to 33 and men to 36, which was normal then. It says violent attack near or just before death was more common for males, and many females died during childbirth. Heavy wear on the teeth suggests a diet rich in meat, with no evidence of vitamin or calcium deficiency and only rare signs of severe starvation. Fireplaces dating between 5000 and 4000 BCE held bones of sheep, goats, cattle and hunted animals such as camels, and there was a large cemetery. The book says the inhabitants had better food than people in later eras, and were fit, tall and strong.

Questions: What two things were stone tools used for? How did people make a stone blade? What time period is the Neolithic Age? What does “Neolithic Age” mean? What did archaeologists study to learn about humans in Jebel Buhais? Complete a table on life expectancy and common reasons for dying for males and females. What animals did people cook in their fireplaces?

Page 132–134 · Reading 5

Activity 9: In groups, think about the ways in which life in Jebel Buhais was good, but difficult. How was life good? How was life difficult? Activity 10: There were many firsts during the Neolithic Age on the Arabian Peninsula. In groups, review the lesson to find the firsts in these areas: tools, shelter, preparing food, the way society lived, trade and pearling. The first one is done for you: people used stone to make arrowheads for hunting and cutting tools.
