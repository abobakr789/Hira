CONCEPT CARD

Moral rules

Moral rules are rules about right and wrong. The book says Japan added a moral programme in 1989 with one hour each week, and that the Earth Charter was launched in 2000. UAE heritage is described as a fence that protects the individual, family and society from values contrary to their own. Corruption is the opposite of morals.

📖 Book text

Student Book Vol. 1 · Student Book readings with dated factual corrections.

Page 38–40 · Reading 1

What are some examples of moral rules? What are some goals of the United Nations? What can people do to help preserve the UAE culture?

Activity 1: Read the poem by Ahmad Shawki in the Student Book. In it, the poet says that lying is the worst thing and that honest people hold on to honesty even when they expect severe consequences. Write a sentence summarising the moral values that the poet supports.

Page 41–42 · Reading 2

Activity 2: The United Nations has always been committed to human dignity and rights, environmental preservation and environmental protection. Sustainable development puts these values into action, both for present and future generations. […] When learning how to build a sustainable future, people need to understand their own values, society’s values and the values of others around the world. The Earth Charter was launched in 2000 by the Earth Charter Commission. It is the outcome of a decade-long global intercultural dialogue on common goals, moral principles and shared values. […] A 1987 UN commission called for a new charter. The Earth Charter initiative was launched by civil-society organisations in 1994; the final charter was launched in June 2000.

Tasks: (a) What moral values do you think are common among individuals all over the world? (b) Research the Earth Charter principles and explain the importance of applying them in societies. (c) Corruption is the opposite of morals. Research the UAE government policy aimed at combating corruption at the level of state departments and summarise the legislation.

Page 42–43 · Reading 3

Activity 3: There is a saying in Japan: “Morals are the secret behind the success of the Japanese experience.” Japanese society is disciplined, creative, respects time and order, values teamwork, and highly committed to ethics and good manners. […] Japanese social values compel people to apologise for mistakes. […] Japan revised its moral-education curriculum in 1989. Its dedicated moral-education period had been introduced in 1958 (MEXT). […] One hour every week throughout the school year is dedicated to a specialized programme on morals. […] Japanese students also clean their schools and care for the plants and animals there.

Tasks: (a) Give an example of community service that you do at your school with your classmates. (b) Analyse the concept of “moral duty”. Then create a scene with a classmate showing the importance of apologising for a mistake.

Page 44–45 · Reading 4

Activity 4: The UAE’s heritage combines spiritual, emotional and behavioural values. Decades ago, the late founder and leader of the state, Sheikh Zayed bin Sultan Al Nahyan (may God have mercy upon him), said that “he who has no past has neither present nor future”. He also said that heritage represents the spirit, wisdom and innovations of nations. […] The culture of globalisation has led to a more open world. The result is that a nation can become influenced by values that are not part of its moral code. Our UAE cultural heritage then becomes a fence that protects the individual, the family and society from cultures that are contrary to our values and morals. […] National popular culture in the UAE aims to introduce new generations to their cultural, moral and civilising roots.

Tasks: (a) Compare the Emirati, Moroccan and German cultural heritages. (b) Research the programme “The Stories of Ancestors, Righteousness” and explain its role in teaching the importance of popular culture to future generations.

Page 46 · Reading 5

Activity 5: Discuss the sayings in the Student Book with your classmates. How would you describe their meanings? The sayings are about treating people according to your own morals, about morality being a mirror of a person, and about good manners being remembered longer than good looks. Question: What moral values can be gained from family and school?
