Page 64–66 · Reading 1

What does it mean to be financially responsible? How can you use money to help other people? Activity 1: We all use money in our daily lives. But what is money? The book says it enables us to buy and sell items, so it is a medium of exchange. List three things that you recently bought. Most people think money is very valuable: what value does money have? As well as being a medium of exchange, the book says money is a means of change: you can use money to change your situation or the situation of others. The book also shows banknotes from different countries and asks: which countries are they from, and what could you buy with the amounts shown?

Page 67–68 · Reading 2

Activity 2: Read about money. Across history, people have exchanged goods directly (barter) and used commodities, metal coins and paper money. These forms overlapped; they were not a universal sequence in which every society passed through identical stages. Commodity money has value as a usable good, while coins and notes make exchange easier when people accept them. Metal money did not end warfare.

Paper money was used in China long before European banknotes. In Europe, Sweden issued banknotes in 1661, and English goldsmiths issued receipts for deposited coins around the same period. Do not treat goldsmith receipts as the worldwide origin of paper money.

Questions: (1) Give an example of barter that is different from the one in the text. (2) Compare and contrast commodity money and paper money. (3) Complete the table for barter, commodity money, metal money and paper money: explanation, difficulties, example.

Page 69–70 · Reading 3

Activity 3: Read “I Earn, I Spend”. The book says to manage your money you need to be financially responsible, and that money is a means of exchange: if you provide a good or a service you receive, or earn, money, and if you want a good or service you spend money. Income can come from investments, such as rent or interest, or from a salary. Income is one side of your money flow; the other is expenditure. Living expenses include rent, groceries, health costs and transport. The book says being financially responsible means balancing income and expenditure and trying not to spend more than you earn.

Planning how to spend is called budgeting. It has a practical side and a moral side: spend wisely and do not waste money. The book gives the “rule of thirds”: spend one third on needs, put another third in a medium-term saving for useful but pricey wants, and the last third in a long-term saving plan. It advises attending to needs before wants, avoiding frivolous purchases, and remembering the ongoing costs of a purchase, such as fuel and repairs for a car. This is one classroom example, not a rule that fits every household. Essential costs and circumstances differ; a budget should fit the actual income and needs.

Questions: (1) What is a short-term goal? (2) What is a long-term goal? (3) Identify one short-term goal and one long-term goal that need balancing your budget. (4) Assume you earn AED 500 per month. Would you still budget your spending to achieve your long-term goal or your short-term goal? Justify your answer.

Page 71–73 · Reading 4

Activity 4: Read “A Cheap Jacket in Paris”. The book says that as teenagers become adults they become more financially independent, so it is important for them to understand financial concepts. In the story, a boy named Ahmad is on holiday in Paris with his family and sees a jacket that costs 100 euros, while at home the same jacket costs 400 dirhams. He thinks it is only a quarter of the price. His father explains that 100 euros is not the same as 100 dirhams, because of the rate of exchange, which is like a translation for money. Most countries have their own currency, like the language of money, and this helps people trade together. In the story, one euro is worth four dirhams, so the jacket costs 400 dirhams.

Tasks: (1) Find out how much each of these currencies is worth in UAE dirhams: euro, dollar, pound sterling, yen, ruble and won. (2) Calculate how much the following are in each currency: a PlayStation console with two controllers, two cans of orange juice, and your school tuition (or a school of your choice).

Page 73–75 · Reading 5

Activity 5: Read “The Power of Money”. The book says the saying “money is the root of all evil” is not the actual expression; the actual one is about the love of money, and that is an important distinction. It says money is a means of exchange, has no inherent value, and is in many ways morally neutral: how you use it gives it moral value. Some people become greedy and want money for its own sake, but money can be used for good.

The book gives three ways money can be a force for good. First, acts of charity, also called philanthropy: helping a friend in difficulty, or donating to a cause. The book says Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum established Noor Dubai to deliver eye care for people with blindness and visual impairment. Second, spending: every purchase helps pay someone’s wages, and “ethical purchasing”, such as Fair Trade coffee, supports values you believe in. Third, setting up a business: it supports the local economy and gives people jobs. Task: identify four ways you could use money as a force for good.

Activity 6: The book asks how well you understand money. When you buy a games console, do you consider ongoing costs like new games? Are you aware of risks? What should you consider in these cases: going to the cinema, your brother buying a motorbike, your father buying an expensive computer, your new phone being stolen, and an email saying you have inherited a large sum of money?
