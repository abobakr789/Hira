📖 Book text

Student Book Vol. 1 · Original text from the Student Book.

Page 162–163 · Reading 1

What effects has colonisation had on Africa? Warm up: Some European languages are widely spoken, or used as official or second languages, in African countries. Try to list countries where a European language is the native language or a commonly spoken language of the people of the country. The book’s box lists English, French, Portuguese, Afrikaans and Spanish. Describe one theory of why some European languages are spoken in Africa.

Page 164–165 · Reading 2

The book says that as Europeans explored more areas outside of Europe in the 1600s, they found Africa, a land rich in natural resources. The land area of Africa is about 30.37 million square kilometres, three times larger than China, and larger than the combined areas of China, Canada and the USA. Africa has inhospitable deserts but also milder areas ideal for growing crops. Natural resources include diamonds, raw materials for sugar, gold, iron, cobalt, uranium, copper, bauxite, silver and petroleum, in addition to agricultural products.

The first Industrial Revolution ranged from the mid 1700s to the mid 1800s. People in European countries needed natural resources to produce goods that could not be found in Europe. At the same time, the population of Europe was high compared to the land available, causing overcrowding, poor living conditions and widespread poverty. Some European powers saw Africa as a way to increase their natural resources and to give economic opportunities to people in their own countries. People from Europe were sent to set up farms, mines and other businesses to send products back. People from different European countries sometimes claimed the same lands, and some disputes led to fighting and wars between them.

Page 165–168 · Reading 3

The book says that in 1884 to 1885, diplomats from Great Britain, Germany, France, Belgium, Italy and Spain met to discuss the situation in Africa. The meeting was called the Berlin Conference because it was held in Berlin, Germany. At this diplomatic conference the countries agreed to divide the continent of Africa among themselves to be ruled as colonies. A colony is a place far away from one country yet ruled as a part of that country. Usually, the laws in a colony are based on the laws of the ruling country, not the country that was colonised.

Activity 1: Answer the questions. According to the text, Africa is about how many square kilometres, and larger than the combined areas of which countries? What were some problems facing Europe in the 18th and 19th centuries? List some of the important natural resources of Africa. What did some European powers want to use Africa for? Why was the Berlin Conference of 1884 to 1885 organised? What was the outcome? Activity 2: Review your discussion in the warm up. What is the relationship between the languages commonly spoken in some African countries today and the European colonial powers? Explain with at least three examples.

Page 169–171 · Reading 4

The book says previous lessons presented past civilizations in Africa. Many were rich and prosperous. At the time that European countries colonised Africa, the ancient civilizations had declined or disappeared, and European powers had more advanced weapons and were able to establish the colonies. The colonial boundaries were very different from the traditional boundaries between peoples and cultures. They often separated people who shared the same culture, language, values and beliefs, and restricted their movement. In other cases, people with very different cultures and languages were included in the same area. These practices often led to conflicts and wars.

Eventually, the European powers withdrew from African colonies. In some cases the colonies became too expensive or difficult to manage; in other cases people in the colonies wanted to set up their own government. The book says only Liberia and Ethiopia were not colonised, that the modern country of Egypt was formed in 1922 although Britain kept control of parts along the Suez Canal until 1956, that most of the rest of Africa received independence after 1956, and that areas of West Africa became independent states in 1960. Activity 3: Combine the information in the text with other information in the lesson to form a conclusion. Were the “new” countries created in Africa after colonisation really “new” countries? Explain your answer. What do you think some of the challenges were for countries which were colonised? List at least three and explain.

Page 172–173 · Reading 5

Activity 4: Match the picture of the leader to the description and name. Write the letter next to the correct description under each picture. The book gives three descriptions. Nelson Mandela was the first black person in his African country to be president. Before his election, his country had a political system called apartheid, in which black Africans had little rights. The book says he was put in prison from 1964 to 1982 for trying to gain rights for black Africans, and that he used his time as president to get people to work together in peace. Haile Selassie was the leader of one of the African countries which was not colonised, and ruled from 1930 to 1974. Patrice Lumumba was the first prime minister of the country formerly called the Belgian Congo and served from June to September 1960.
