Page 162–163 · Reading 1

What effects has colonisation had on Africa? Warm up: Some European languages are widely spoken, or used as official or second languages, in African countries. Try to list countries where a European language is the native language or a commonly spoken language of the people of the country. The book’s box lists English, French, Portuguese, Afrikaans and Spanish. Describe one theory of why some European languages are spoken in Africa.

Page 164–165 · Reading 2

Africa had long-established societies and international connections before European colonial expansion. European powers sought control of its resources and trade routes. The land area of Africa is about 30.37 million square kilometres, three times larger than China, and larger than the combined areas of China, Canada and the USA. Africa has inhospitable deserts but also milder areas ideal for growing crops. Natural resources include diamonds, raw materials for sugar, gold, iron, cobalt, uranium, copper, bauxite, silver and petroleum, in addition to agricultural products.

The first Industrial Revolution ranged from the mid 1700s to the mid 1800s. People in European countries needed natural resources to produce goods that could not be found in Europe. At the same time, the population of Europe was high compared to the land available, causing overcrowding, poor living conditions and widespread poverty. Some European powers saw Africa as a way to increase their natural resources and to give economic opportunities to people in their own countries. People from Europe were sent to set up farms, mines and other businesses to send products back. People from different European countries sometimes claimed the same lands, and some disputes led to fighting and wars between them.

Page 165–168 · Reading 3

The book says that in 1884 to 1885, diplomats from Great Britain, Germany, France, Belgium, Italy and Spain met to discuss the situation in Africa. The meeting was called the Berlin Conference because it was held in Berlin, Germany. The conference set rules for colonial claims and trade, without African representatives. It did not draw all of Africa's borders; many were imposed later through conquest and agreements between colonial powers. A colony is a place far away from one country yet ruled as a part of that country. Usually, the laws in a colony are based on the laws of the ruling country, not the country that was colonised.

Activity 1: Answer the questions. According to the text, Africa is about how many square kilometres, and larger than the combined areas of which countries? What were some problems facing Europe in the 18th and 19th centuries? List some of the important natural resources of Africa. What did some European powers want to use Africa for? Why was the Berlin Conference of 1884 to 1885 organised? What was the outcome? Activity 2: Review your discussion in the warm up. What is the relationship between the languages commonly spoken in some African countries today and the European colonial powers? Explain with at least three examples.

Page 169–171 · Reading 4

European conquest met existing African states and societies and organised resistance. Military power and rivalries helped colonial expansion; Africa was not an empty continent without political life. The colonial boundaries were very different from the traditional boundaries between peoples and cultures. They often separated people who shared the same culture, language, values and beliefs, and restricted their movement. In other cases, people with very different cultures and languages were included in the same area. These practices often led to conflicts and wars.

Eventually, the European powers withdrew from African colonies. In some cases the colonies became too expensive or difficult to manage; in other cases people in the colonies wanted to set up their own government. Liberia and Ethiopia are commonly named as exceptions to long-term European colonial rule, but Italy occupied Ethiopia from 1936 to 1941. The textbook describes Egypt's independence in 1922 and British control along the Suez Canal until 1956. Many West African countries became independent in 1960; Ghana was already independent on 6 March 1957. Activity 3: Combine the information in the text with other information in the lesson to form a conclusion. Were the “new” countries created in Africa after colonisation really “new” countries? Explain your answer. What do you think some of the challenges were for countries which were colonised? List at least three and explain.

Page 172–173 · Reading 5

Activity 4: Match the picture of the leader to the description and name. Write the letter next to the correct description under each picture. The book gives three descriptions. Nelson Mandela was the first black person in his African country to be president. Before his election, his country had a political system called apartheid, in which black Africans had little rights. Verified correction: he was imprisoned from 1962 to 1990 during the struggle against apartheid; 1964 to 1982 was his main period on Robben Island, not his entire imprisonment, and he used his time as president to get people to work together in peace. Haile Selassie was emperor of Ethiopia from 1930 to 1974, with an interruption during the Italian occupation of 1936 to 1941. Patrice Lumumba was the first prime minister of the country formerly called the Belgian Congo and served from June to September 1960.
