📖 Book text 1/6

What is addiction? Addiction and habit

Read and explain.

Why do you think people become addicted to bad habits? What do you think are some of the symptoms of an addiction?

Addiction is a global issue that many different societies deal with on an increasing scale. When we consider addiction, most people think of being addicted to drugs, tobacco or alcohol. However, addictions can also be linked to activities such as gambling, playing video games, shopping and technology.

Addiction is a psychological condition in which the individual uses a substance, or indulges in an activity which can result in short term pleasure, but which the body or the individual comes to depend on. The behaviour becomes compulsive and begins to interfere with their normal daily functioning, their social relationships and their health. People with an addiction may not appreciate that their behaviour is out of control, nor recognise the implications of their behaviour.

There is a difference between an addiction and a habit. […] Habits can become addictions. However, most of us have habitual behaviour, but are not addicts.

📖 Book text 2/6

Habit, dependence and control

Read and compare.

The crucial point is the consequences of behaviour. When the behaviour becomes compulsive and the body or mind become dependent on the behaviour, then the long term effects can become damaging. This is why addictive behaviour can be dangerous. With habits, people can often choose to stop, and will stop if they want to. This is not the same as an addiction. If you are addicted to something, it means that you face more challenges in controlling the addiction without help because of the mental and physical aspects involved. You are not as much in control of your choices.

Many addictive substances […] the body feels a sense of euphoria. […] the person will need to take more of the drug to maintain the effect because the body builds a tolerance to the drug. The person may develop intense cravings for the substance and will continue to use it, despite the harmful and dangerous consequences.

In the World Drug Report 2017, the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime estimates that there were “29.5 million people globally suffering from drug use disorders”. There are different kinds of addictions, but the most prevalent ones globally are alcohol, tobacco and drugs.

Student Book pp. 160–161 (selected passages). Research Task (p. 161): find four addictive substances and notes on what makes them addictive. Teacher checks sources; use reliable health sites only.

📖 Book text 3/6

Psychological risk factors and stress

Read and list.

There are no set rules for why addiction develops in some people, but not others. […] Having a risk factor does not mean that a person will develop an addiction. However, the more risk factors there are, the greater the potential for addictive behaviours to develop.

Two personality characteristics, sensation seeking and impulsiveness, have been linked or associated with different types of addiction. Sensation-seeking involves taking risks in the pursuit of pleasure and excitement, and impulsiveness is behaving in a way that has little or no forethought, reflection or consideration of the consequences.

In the modern world, stress is part of everyday life […]. Stress is the way that your body responds to any kind of demand or threat and affects both the mind and the body. […] When people find it difficult to deal with stress, they may take drugs or resort to other forms of behaviour to help them feel better and gain pleasure and release from their stress. If they do turn to these behaviours, they can become addictive.

It is important to remember that even though people may have these risk factors, it does not mean that they will become addicted or exhibit addictive behaviour.

Student Book Vol. 1 · page 161–163 · Reading 3

Student Book pp. 161–163 (selected passages). Stress is something everyone has. Healthy ways to cope are not on these book pages; teachers may add general advice (talk to someone, rest, exercise) as our own words.

📖 Book text 4/6

Biological, environmental and peer risk factors

Read and discuss.

Many of our earliest interactions in life affect our development. Strong and healthy relationships with family and friends are vital as they form the basis of children’s development. Ineffective parenting and a chaotic home environment, with a lack of strong relationships with a caring adult, can all increase the risk factors. […] Research has shown that “environmental factors influenced the tendency to addiction more than individual factors.”

Having strong relationships and bonds within families provides a safer environment for children and reduces the risk factors associated with addictive behaviour.

Peer influence is not a cause of addiction, but, combined with other risk factors, still has the potential to lead to addictive behaviour. Peer pressure can be a positive or a negative thing. Peers can provide positive role models for each other. If a friend asks you to help on a volunteer project to plant more trees in the local park or school, this is a positive influence. […]

However, it is important to remember that peers can have a negative influence on you as well. If a peer wants you to try smoking a cigarette or something illegal, like drugs or alcohol, remember to tell them “No!” immediately and tell them to stop as well. Peer pressure can be powerful but resisting peer pressure and making wise and sensible decisions demonstrates self-control and independence of mind.

Student Book pp. 163–164 (selected passages). Be sensitive: some students may have difficult home situations. Do not ask anyone to share family details.

📖 Book text 5/6

Potential symptoms of addiction

Read and discuss.

Addiction affects a person’s brain. This can develop into a situation where the person cannot control the use of the substance. According to the Mayo Clinic in the United States, depending on the type of drug being abused, the symptoms will be different. Drug addiction symptoms or behaviours can include the following:

(1) A feeling that you need to use the drug or carry out an activity daily. (2) Having very strong cravings […] that block out everything else. (3) Over a period of time, needing more of the drug or activity to get the same effect. […] (5) Not meeting obligations and work responsibilities, or reducing the amount of social and recreational activities. (6) Continuing […] even though you know it is causing problems in your life or causing you psychological harm. […] (10) Failing in your attempts to stop using the drug or pursuing the activity. (11) Experiencing withdrawal symptoms when you attempt to stop […]. Withdrawal symptoms are the unpleasant physical reactions that happen when an addict stops taking an addictive drug.

Discussion Point (p. 165): What would you do if you spotted the symptoms of addiction in a friend? Do these symptoms mean that your friend is showing symptoms of addictive behaviour, or are they an escalation to addiction? Discuss whether these types of behaviour are proof of the development of addictive behaviours.

Student Book Vol. 1 · page 164–166 · Reading 5

Student Book pp. 164–166 (selected items from the list of 11). Guide the discussion towards caring and getting help: speak to the friend kindly and tell a trusted adult. Teachers should not give a diagnosis; symptoms are not proof.

📖 Book text 6/6

Risk and protective factors; self-assessment

Read, sort, and answer.

Action Task (p. 166): Look at the terms provided and then categorise them into ‘risk factors’ or ‘protective factors’. Compare your answers with your group. Can you think of two more factors for each category? The terms are: aggressive childhood behaviour, community poverty, school anti-drug policies, positive relationships.

Self-Assessment Task (p. 167): (1) Why are the consequences of ‘addiction’ seen in both developed and developing countries? Give reasons for your answer. (2) If a person has a lot of ‘risk factors’ in their life, does this mean that they will definitely develop an ‘addiction’? Why or why not? (3) Why are ‘protective factors’ important in reducing the risk of addictive behaviours occurring? Give reasons for your answer.
