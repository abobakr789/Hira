CONCEPT CARD

Effects of addiction

Addiction can harm physical and mental health (for example insomnia, anxiety and psychosis), family trust, friendships, school and work, and personal finances. Societies pay through rehabilitation clinics, helplines, policing and health care. Treatment with respect and care helps recovery.

Addiction affects everyone, and care helps recovery.

Words to know

insomniaan inability to sleepcompulsiona strong urge to do something again and again
psychosisseeing or perceiving reality in a different way from what is classed as normaldenialrefusing to accept that there is a problem
rehabilitation clinica place that treats and supports people recovering from addictiondebtmoney owed to someone
paranoiafeeling you are in danger even though it is highly unlikelyrelapsegoing back to the habit after trying to stop

Page 168–171 · Reading 1

What effects can an addiction have on physical wellbeing? What effects can an addiction have on mental wellbeing?

Addictive substances affect all parts of your body, how your body functions and cause health problems that could remain with you for the rest of your life. Drugs affect many parts of the brain, which lead to problems with emotional health, learning and sleeping. […] generating insomnia (an inability to sleep), and paranoia (the thinking and feeling that you are in personal danger or under threat even though it is highly unlikely that this is the case) among other problems.

Substance addiction can lead to heart disease, cancer, lung disease, a weakened immune system and damage your liver. It can also cause brain damage, memory problems, attention disorders and decision-making problems. Addictions can also cause a number of mental problems contributing to depression, and over arousal, anxiety, schizophrenia, a feeling of not being in control and psychosis (seeing or perceiving reality in a different way to that which would be classed as ‘normal’).

One of the consequences of mental health issues caused by substance addiction is that it makes feeling and showing empathy, and developing strong relationships more difficult to do. When people are addicted to a substance, they may take more risks and find it difficult to think clearly.

Page 170–171 · Reading 2

There are also activities that can become physically and mentally addictive, and the behaviours are similar to those associated with addiction to substances such as drugs or alcohol. Research has shown that work, social media, gambling, shopping and exercise are all activities that have the potential to become addictive.

Similar to substance addiction, the person can become obsessed with the activity and seek it out […]. The person may exhibit denial of the issue, try to hide it from family and friends, and, in the case of addictive shopping behaviour or gambling, can develop serious financial problems. There is an element of compulsion to do the activity repeatedly, even if the individual does not want to do it. If they stop the activity, withdrawal symptoms can appear and feelings of anxiety, irritability and craving can all make the chances of stopping more difficult.

Addiction to social media can exhibit many of these symptoms […]. Using social media can involve sitting down and not moving for long periods of time, which, as you have explored in previous lessons, has physical consequences. […] Sedentary behaviours, as we have seen, have the potential to develop into serious diseases such as diabetes and heart disease.

Page 171–173 · Reading 3

Not only does addiction harm the self, but it affects family members as well. Trust between the addict and their family starts to disappear, and this can cause a lot of tension and family arguments, creating a negative home environment for all. […] Some families may choose to ignore the addict’s problems and pretend that there are no problems at all, because they find it difficult to confront the problem and seek support and help. This may be due to cultural reasons, feeling that such problems bring shame on the family, or that they will be viewed negatively by others.

Addicts may lie and try to hide their problems from their families. Addicts may find it difficult to be responsible and reliable and they can forget things because they are focused on feeding their addiction.

If there is an addict in the family home, this can cause potential risks for younger members of the family. […] when children are in environments where addicts are present, the risk of them developing addiction problems of their own later in life greatly increases.

Action Task (p. 173): draw a mind map to show possible consequences that addiction can have on physical and mental wellbeing. Compare your ideas with your group.

Page 173–175 · Reading 4

Addicts may lose friends who do not want to spend time with them because of their addiction and behaviour. […] Addicts can start to show less enthusiasm and motivation for activities and sports that they used to enjoy.

Action Task (p. 174): make notes about three impacts that addiction has on friends and recreation. With your group, design a poster based on your ideas.

The impacts of addiction do not stop within the family and friends circle, but affect the school and work spheres of life as well. An ambitious and efficient student or worker can change into someone who is late, has poor personal hygiene and behaves inappropriately. […] Addicts tend to focus on feeding their addiction. This means that decision-making can be poor and they are unable to work effectively. In many cases, addicts at school either drop out, or are asked to leave. Workers who are addicts risk losing their jobs.

Discussion Point (p. 175): What would you do if you knew that a peer at school was addicted to a substance? Discuss your ideas with your group and make notes of at least three possible solutions.

Page 175–177 · Reading 5

Constantly trying to get and pay for addictive substances and activities is a priority for many addicts. […] paying for illicit substances or carrying out addictive activities costs money. If an addict has lost their job and has no income, there are limited choices in where they can find the finances to support their addiction. They can borrow money, which quickly turns into a large debt as they have no money to pay back the initial loan. […] This can also lead to them getting pulled further into a spiral of debt, crime, and abuse.

In some countries, there have been attempts to research how much crime is influenced by addiction. In the UK, for example, research suggests that between a third and half of all recorded acquisitive crime is linked to illegal drug use. […]

In terms of societal costs, the problems created by addictions of all kinds must be dealt with. This can include costs associated with the provision of drug addiction helplines, support groups and rehabilitation clinics. Increased funds are needed in terms of policing and dealing with antisocial behaviour linked to addictions, as well as addiction-related crime. Health care costs also increase because of the volume of addiction-related diseases that need to be treated. According to VeryWell Mind, an online advice and support organisation, the cost of illegal drug use in the United States is around $193 billion (709 billion AED).

Page 177–181 · Reading 6

Research Task (p. 178): read the story about Abdullah, a recovering addict who attended the National Rehabilitation Centre (NRC) in Abu Dhabi. Make notes on the consequences that addiction had on his life. Write a 250 word summary about Abdullah’s experiences.

In the story, Abdullah says his addiction began at 22 and carried on for 23 continuous years, he ended up in jail several times, and an earlier treatment attempt failed. At the NRC he was told that his illness requires treatment and that he was neither a criminal nor devious. “Abdullah has been a fully recovered person for the last three and a half years and continues to follow treatment programmes which help him maintain his recovery.” (summary of pp. 178–179; the full story is in the Student Book.)
