Page 135–137 · Reading 1

What made the Umm an-Nar culture unique in how it treated people and in its economic development? What do we know about the Bronze Age? How can equality be improved between people? Activity 1: Read the text. The book says around 5,000 BCE the people of the UAE had a good diet and were generally healthy, but after 4,000 BCE the climate became drier, and evidence from skeletons shows life became very tough. The Indian Ocean monsoon began to move south to its current position, so the area received less rain, and fewer people lived there.

The book says people still grew crops, and the earliest evidence of crop growing is from Hili in Al Ain, in about 3,000 BCE. One of the most important crops was the date palm, which gave food and materials for building and basket making; people also grew wheat and barley. Because rain was not enough, they dug wells and poured water into channels to carry it to the fields. People still gathered wild plants and fruit and hunted animals such as oryx, gazelles and wild camels, and coastal villagers developed ways of catching large fish. Pastoral nomads moved with sheep and goats, probably trading with villages like Hili; by 3,000 BCE donkeys carried goods along the coast and mountain edges, but crossing the sand dunes was still difficult until the camel was domesticated.

Questions: Compare the health of people from 5000 BCE with those from 4000 BCE. What is the probable cause? How did the change in climate change where people lived? Name three ways people obtained food. How did people obtain fresh water? Why was it still difficult to travel from Al Ain across the desert?

Page 138–139 · Reading 2

Activity 2: Read “Umm an-Nar and the Bronze Age”. The book says the society in the UAE area around 2,500 BCE is often called the “Umm an-Nar Culture”, because archaeologists first learned about it on the island of Umm an-Nar near Abu Dhabi. Shared tombs suggest communal identity and broad inclusion. This is evidence to interpret, not proof that everyone had equal status in daily life or that neighbouring societies were uniformly unequal.

The Bronze Age was not one culture or a single starting date everywhere. In the UAE, the Hafit period preceded Umm an-Nar. During the Bronze Age, people used bronze, a mixture of copper and other metals, more and more instead of stone for tools and weapons. The al-Hajar mountains contain massive amounts of copper, which people learned to mine and turn into pure copper.

Page 139–141 · Reading 3

The book says the city-states and kingdoms of Mesopotamia, needed copper to make weapons and agricultural tools, but had none, so merchants came to the Umm an-Nar society to trade for it. Records from Mesopotamia call the area that is now the UAE and Oman the “Magan” society. One text from just after the Umm an-Nar period records a single shipment of over 18 tonnes of copper. The book says merchants brought beautiful ceramic vessels, ivory combs and decorated beads, examples of which were found in Umm an-Nar tombs.

The book says the Umm an-Nar culture flourished for at least 500 years, from about 2500 to 2000 BCE, and established patterns of life, such as trade and agriculture, which still exist in the UAE today, and offers evidence for discussing shared burial practices and social organisation. The extent of equality remains an interpretation, not a fact that can be measured directly from the tombs alone.

Questions: In what two ways was the Umm an-Nar culture unique? What is bronze? What is bronze used for? How were the Umm an-Nar people able to benefit economically from bronze? With whom did they trade copper? What did they receive in return? What patterns of life that still exist today were established then?

Page 141–142 · Reading 4

Research context: A 2024 experimental Magan boat project used bundles of reeds tied with date-palm fibre rope around wooden frames, with a bitumen coating for waterproofing. The reconstruction draws on archaeological evidence and tests how an ancient-style vessel could sail and carry cargo. It is a modern experiment, not a surviving Bronze Age boat. Questions: Which materials serve the hull, support and waterproofing roles? What can a successful reconstruction show, and what can it not prove about every ancient voyage?
