Page 170–172 · Reading 1

What do we know about Ed-Dur? How did travelling affect the spread of diseases? Activity 1: Read “Ed-Dur”. The book says the ancient settlement of Ed-Dur was in what is now Umm al-Quwain, near a large lagoon that opened to the Arabian Gulf. The lagoon was open to the sea and gave ships a natural harbour, so like Mleiha, Ed-Dur was a centre of trade. The main part of the town probably lay a little away from the coast, perhaps for fear of piracy. People lived in and around Ed-Dur for thousands of years; from around 100 CE the population began to increase, and people lived in houses made from locally available stone.

The book says the people of Ed-Dur became wealthy from trade across the Indian Ocean and the Arabian Gulf. Ships from Yemen and India provided spices and incense unavailable in the UAE, and traders from Persia and Mesopotamia brought highly decorated pottery and metal goods. A large amount of material came from the Mediterranean, including jars from the island of Rhodes and Greek and Roman pottery, and bronze and ceramic goods such as ladles, strainers and bowls used for special occasions. Many coins from Ed-Dur show their increasing use for exchange over long distances, and locally minted coins were traded for coins from afar, including the Roman Empire.

Page 172–174 · Reading 2

Questions: In what present-day Emirate is Ed-Dur? What kind of area was Ed-Dur? What was probably the reason Ed-Dur was not right on the coast? When did the population begin to increase? What kinds of goods came from Yemen and India, from Persia and Mesopotamia, and from the Mediterranean? How did the increase in trading affect the population?

Activity 2: Like in the time of Ed-Dur, the UAE still is a centre for trade. It exports and imports many goods. In groups, try to list 3 main exports and 3 main imports of the UAE. You can do some research on this through the Internet or other sources.

Page 175–177 · Reading 3

Activity 3: Read about Dibba. The book says Dibba, in present-day Fujairah, was famous as a hub for international trade in the early Islamic period (approximately the 600s CE). It says recent excavations by the Sharjah Archaeology Authority revealed new evidence that Dibba’s importance as a trading centre stretches back at least 2,000 years: archaeologists excavated a large tomb containing decorated ceramics from Persia and Mesopotamia and glass vessels from Rome.

The book says the skeletons in the tomb give information about the lives and deaths of the people of Dibba, and that careful analysis of the bones indicates that very serious diseases affected some of the people buried there. Two skeletons, one from the tomb itself and one of a poorer person buried nearby, show that tuberculosis may have been present in the population.

Page 177–179 · Reading 4

The book says tuberculosis is a common disease in which the lungs are infected with bacteria that cause coughing, internal bleeding and fevers. Most people who had tuberculosis died because no treatment was available to fight the bacteria. It says the disease spreads primarily from the coughing and sneezing of people living close to each other, so a trading ship would be an ideal environment for it to spread, which may explain its presence in Dibba. The busy port would likely have been home to merchants from Iraq, south Asia, Egypt and Yemen, who may have travelled to places such as Palestine, Greece, Italy and perhaps as far as Spain. Tuberculosis can live in the body for many years, so it could spread very quickly across the known world, and the long-distance trade that brought luxury goods to Dibba could have brought disease as well.

Questions: Why do archaeologists think that Dibba was a trade centre for at least 2,000 years? What did researchers learn by studying skeletons in the tombs? What are the symptoms of tuberculosis? What happens to most people with tuberculosis if they don’t receive treatment? How is tuberculosis spread from one person to another? Why would a trading ship be ideal to spread tuberculosis?

Page 179–181 · Reading 5

Activity 4: Using information from the reading and other sources, compare and contrast the symptoms of tuberculosis and COVID-19, and how each disease spreads through travelling. Activity 5: The people of our world are increasingly interconnected. How can we prevent diseases from spreading through travel in the future? Discuss with other students. Activity 6: Since the emergence of COVID-19, life has changed for everyone. How has life changed for you? Write your thoughts.
