CONCEPT CARD

Ancient Mesopotamia

Mesopotamia lay between the Tigris and the Euphrates, in the Fertile Crescent. The Sumerians, Akkadians (Sargon), Babylon (Hammurabi), Assyrians and Neo-Babylonians (Nebuchadnezzar II) ruled in turn until Cyrus II and Alexander the Great.

Mesopotamia shaped early civilisation.

Page 241–243 · Reading 1

Have you ever heard of Babylon? The picture shows an artist’s depiction of what the gates of the city might have looked like. Work with three or four other students to find three facts about ancient Babylon.

Mesopotamia means the land between the Tigris and Euphrates. It is centered on present-day Iraq and extends into parts of Syria and Turkey. It is part of the broader Fertile Crescent, not another name for all of it. Irrigation and river-borne soil supported farming and the growth of settlements.

Page 243–244 · Reading 2

The Sumerians were the first great society of the Mesopotamian region. With their invention of cuneiform, their knowledge of agriculture, and their trade with neighbouring peoples, the Sumerians developed a wealthy stable society that controlled Mesopotamia until it was conquered by the Akkadians around 2300 BCE.

The Akkadian king, Sargon, established the world’s first empire as he gained control of the entire Mesopotamian region. He established trade routes throughout his kingdom and traded with people from the neighbouring Indus Valley region of India. Sargon’s focus on developing the economy of Mesopotamia brought his kingdom great wealth.

A later ruler, King Ur-Nammu, established and recorded the laws and administrative processes of the kingdom around 2100 BCE. The Sumerian language was reintroduced, many destroyed buildings were rebuilt, and a standard measurement system was developed that helped trade to flourish. This new stability and success contributed to the foundation for another early civilisation: Babylon.

Page 244 · Reading 3

Hammurabi ruled Babylon in the eighteenth century BCE. He was an important king of the First Babylonian Dynasty, not its founder. Through alliances and military campaigns he expanded Babylon’s power. The dynasty and its rulers must not be collapsed into one event.

Hammurabi was an effective ruler and military leader. A code of laws developed at that time, called the Code of Hammurabi, was inscribed on a large black stone. It can still be seen today, in the Louvre Museum in Paris, France.

The Code records legal rules, penalties and claims to protect vulnerable people. Punishments differed by social status. Do not treat it as a modern equal-rights legal system or a simple statement of today’s presumption of innocence.

Page 245 · Reading 4

Assyrian power developed over several periods. Babylon was not ruled by one Assyrian dynasty continuously for 1,400 years from 2000 BCE. Neo-Assyrian rule over Babylonia faced repeated revolts; Sennacherib sacked Babylon in 689 BCE, and later rulers rebuilt it.

Nabopolassar restored Babylonian independence in the late seventh century BCE. His son Nebuchadnezzar II ruled in the early sixth century BCE and rebuilt Babylon, including the Ishtar Gate and Processional Way. The Hanging Gardens remain debated, with no securely identified remains at Babylon.

Page 245–246 · Reading 5

Several kings ruled between Nebuchadnezzar II and Nabonidus, the last Neo-Babylonian king. Cyrus II conquered Babylon in 539 BCE. Achaemenid rule lasted until Alexander’s conquest in 331 BCE, about two centuries, not almost one century. Babylon remained important before gradually losing influence to newer centers such as Seleucia.

Achaemenid rule over Babylon lasted from 539 to 331 BCE, about two centuries. Alexander then took Babylon. Later, Seleucia gradually displaced it as an important regional center.

Over the past two hundred years, archaeologists have discovered a wide variety of artifacts and ancient texts from the Mesopotamian civilisations. These objects have given researchers a detailed picture of Mesopotamian history, political organization, art, and architecture. Ongoing archaeological and historical research continues to add more depth and breadth to our understanding of human past.
