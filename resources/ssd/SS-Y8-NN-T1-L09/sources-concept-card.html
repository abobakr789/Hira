📖 Book text

Student Book Vol. 1 · Original text from the Student Book.

Page 85–86 · Reading 1

What can you do to show respect and empathy for people from other cultures? Activity 1: To overcome discrimination, we need to respect diversity and difference and be respectful of those less fortunate than us. People from a very poor economic background do not always receive education. People with disabilities cannot always access transport or employment. These people often experience discrimination. Imagine being a refugee fleeing for your life from a war-torn country, afraid for your life and the lives of your family. You arrive in a community where you are not made to feel welcome.

Questions: (a) How would you feel? (b) What could people do to make you feel more welcome?

Page 86–87 · Reading 2

Activity 2: You will now have a chance to empathise with people in unfortunate situations. The book gives four scenarios with fictional names. (1) Fredo came to a new country but cannot speak the language, is put in a younger grade and has a hard time fitting in. (2) Fatima lost her parents in a war and moved in with her uncle’s family. She misses her parents and old home, and people treat her as strange and are afraid to approach her because of her sad story. (3) Amanda lives with 12 other family members in a small, crowded home, finds it hard to do her homework, and gets in trouble at school. (4) Samer has a hearing disability, finds it hard to take part in class, and is often excluded by other students.

When you have read your assigned scenario, consider with your group: (a) What prejudice or discrimination is taking place? (b) How can we help?

Page 87–90 · Reading 3

Activity 3: Can you think of any famous people who have spoken out against discrimination and prejudice? The book introduces Dr. Martin Luther King, Jr. (USA, 1929–1968), an outspoken American activist who protested against discrimination against African-Americans. He became the leader of the American Civil Rights movement and won the Nobel Peace Prize. He raised worldwide attention about segregation, the exclusion of African-Americans from many aspects of mainstream society. The book says he experienced segregation as a boy and as a teenager. Perhaps his most famous speech was on 28 August 1963, from the Lincoln Memorial in Washington, when he told the world that he had a dream of a future free from prejudice and discrimination.

Questions: (a) Why do you think King became such a passionate campaigner for equality? (b) How would you feel if you had to go to a different school because of the colour of your skin? (c) If you were to write a speech about equality and inclusion today, what would you write? Activity 4: Consider the quotes from Martin Luther King in the book. (a) Analyse each quote. (b) How would you apply each quote in view of tolerance?

Page 90–91 · Reading 4

Activity 5: Consider how people of determination have been discriminated against. “He will never write!” These words, which James Cawley faced from a very early age, made him more determined than ever to succeed. James comes from a small village in Ireland and was born with a condition that affects the joints. The book says he uses a wheelchair in everyday life and has very limited use of his arms and hands. He has proven prejudice wrong time and time again. The doctors were wrong: he did write. He broke down the barriers of prejudice, including the assumptions that he could not go to university or live away from home. He graduated with a degree in Geography and Business and is now a teacher. With the help of personal assistants, he thrived. The book lists some of his achievements, such as leading an online awareness campaign and being featured on national television about poor accessibility.

Questions: (a) How do you feel when you read James’s story? (b) What thoughts come into your head when you see a person of determination? (c) How can you help people of determination feel less excluded? The book also asks you to read a letter James wrote to himself while at university: does it inspire you? Why?

Page 92–95 · Reading 5

Activity 6: In the UAE, the book says people of determination enjoy a special place in government policy, and every effort is made to ensure that any form of disability, mental or physical, is not discriminated against. The national policy is based on six aspects of society: (1) health and rehabilitation, (2) education, (3) vocational rehabilitation and employment, (4) outreach, including building regulations that take account of special needs, (5) social protection and family empowerment, including the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, and (6) public life, culture and sports.

Questions: Consider the six aspects. In what ways could people of determination experience discrimination if these measures were not in place? Research: (1) Find an organisation that fights discrimination and promotes equality. (2) Look at the charter of human rights and identify how these rights are connected to prejudice and discrimination. (3) Identify a celebrity who has fought for equality and against discrimination.

Let’s talk

① What does the book say people from a very poor background do not always receive? ② Who is described as an American Civil Rights leader, in the book? ③ How many aspects does the UAE policy have, in the book?
