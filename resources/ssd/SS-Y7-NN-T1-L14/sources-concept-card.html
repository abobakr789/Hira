Page 160–162 · Reading 1

Muslim ambassadors and teachers reached western China by about 650 CE, only 18 years after the death of the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him). Between about 742 and 758 CE, the first major settlements of Muslims in China had flourished. A community of Muslims with a mosque was established at Xi’an in western China. At that time, the city of Xi’an was known as Chang’an and was the capital city of the Tang Dynasty. […] Over the following centuries, Muslim influence spread through merchants who prospered along the Silk Route. […] Some Muslim names were assimilated within the Chinese language. For example, the name Li is sometimes a shortened version of the Muslim name Ali and Ma is sometimes a shortened version of the name Mohammad. According to the 2000 Chinese National Census, there were over 23 million Muslims in China. Most Chinese Muslims live in China’s western provinces.

Questions: (a) When did the first Muslims arrive in China? (b) This followed 18 years after which event? (c) Which two Chinese names could be short for Muslim names? What were they short for? (d) How was Islam spread in western China? (e) How many Chinese Muslims were estimated to be in China in the 2000 census?

Page 163–165 · Reading 2

Activity 2: Evidence of communities of early Muslims can be found in China. Mosque 1: the Huajuexiang Mosque in Xi’an, known as the Great Mosque of Xi’an, is one of the oldest and best-preserved mosques from the early period of Islam in China. It is thought to have been founded by the great naval Admiral Zheng He. The mosque we find now was begun in 1392, when the Ming Dynasty was young. Mosque 2: the Atikar Mosque in Kashi, Xinjiang Province, was built during the Ming Dynasty in western China, in the 1440s, and opens onto a large public square called the Festival Square. Mosque 3: the Amin Mosque and Minaret in Xinjiang Province, built along the Silk Road in 1779 during the Qing Dynasty, is distinguished by China’s tallest minaret and a fortified appearance. Its brick patterns and arched niches show influences from Persia and Central Asia.

Questions: (a) Which mosque looks the most like other Chinese buildings of the time? (b) Which mosque looks the most like a fort? (c) Which mosque looks the most like a palace? Describe and say why. (d) Challenge: why do you think the style of mosques was so different? (think of time, situation, etc.)

Page 166–169 · Reading 3

Activity 3: Indianisation refers to the diffusion of Indian cultural and social influence on Southeast Asia. It includes the spread of Indian language, philosophy, and social culture. Southeast Asian cultures adapted Indian styles of clothing and architecture to their pre-existing culture, making something similar to Indian culture yet still their own distinct style. An example is the Angkor Wat complex of the Khmer empire in present-day Cambodia. Social scientists have three theories. The first theory is that Indian culture spread through military conquest in the early middle age period. The second theory states that merchants spread Indian culture, beliefs and practices. The third theory suggests that Indian culture was spread by people seeking to spread their religion.

Questions: (a) What is Indianisation? (b) What is spread through the process of Indianisation? (c) What is a theory? (d) Match the categories economical, religious and political to their descriptions: through political alliances or military conquest; through seeking to make money; through people accepting religious beliefs.

Page 170–171 · Reading 4

Activity 4: The images show Indianisation in architecture. Look at the images and complete the tasks. Image: Angkor Wat, built in the early 12th century in Cambodia. (a) List 5 features of the structure. (b) Why do you think the complex had elements of different styles of architecture?

Page 172–175 · Reading 5

Activity 5: Islam was introduced into Southeast Asia by Muslim merchants and religious people. Teachers, traders and politicians were accepted within royal court culture. Islam provided an ideology and ethical system that promoted fair trade and commercial activity. When Islam was accepted by the rulers and noble class, Islamic cultural influences spread to villages. As Islam spread to Java, Sumatra and other parts of Southeast Asia, it appealed not only to nobles but to common people for its ideology of individual worth and equality. […] By 1511 the Portuguese had conquered the Malacca region. […] Islamic culture is the dominant culture in Indonesia and Malaysia up to modern times.

Questions: (a) How did Islam arrive in Southeast Asia? (b) Why did Islam appeal to people in these countries? (c) Was the spread of Islamic culture mostly political, economic, or religious? Give a reason from the text. (d) How did the Portuguese presence affect the spread of Islamic culture? Activity 6: Explain the meaning of two statements from the text and say why these ideas would be attractive to the people of Southeast Asia.
