CONCEPT CARD

Benefits and costs of globalisation

Benefits: more variety and quality, lower prices, movement of labour, capital and funds, multinational investment, greater awareness. Costs: inequality, no trickle-down effect, harm to local businesses, risk to culture, working conditions, the environment. A good judgement weighs both sides with reasons.

Weigh benefits and costs, then give a reasoned judgement.

Words to know

capitalany item produced to help produce other goods and servicesinequalitya gap between rich and poor (the lesson asks whether it grows)
capital goodsitems such as cranes, servers, office buildings, ships and lorriesdiversifyto avoid relying on one or two key products
multinational companya company with its headquarters in one country and operations in othersinfrastructurefacilities such as roads, ports and airports
trickle-down effectthe idea that wealth created by investment flows down to everyone in a countrywater scarcea country that does not have much water

Think and talk

① Name a benefit. ② Name a cost. ③ Which is greater, and why?

Page 28–29 · Reading 1

Can you think of some advantages and disadvantages of globalisation? What do you think a multi-national business is? Can you name some multi-national businesses operating in the UAE?

We have seen that globalisation is associated with the increase in the connections between countries, which has meant that trade between countries has increased over the last 50 years. […] Many students, for example, would recognise the Emirates brand even if they have never been on an Emirates flight. […]

Like any process, globalisation can bring many benefits to the people of the world but it can also result in costs. In this lesson, we are going to explore some of these benefits and costs.

Page 30–31 · Reading 2

An increase in the variety and quality of goods and services. One of the main benefits of globalisation has been the increase in trade between nations. This has meant that millions of people around the world have access to a much greater variety of goods and services and in many cases, goods and services which are of high quality. For example, people can buy different types of fruit and vegetables all year round, not just when they are in season.

Availability at lower prices. The increase in global trade has also made many goods available to more people at lower prices. […]

Movement of labour. Look around the UAE and you will see many different people from many different backgrounds. […] Many of these people who come to work in the UAE bring with them skills, innovative ideas and expertise which can benefit the businesses they work for and also the country. They also bring a richness of cultural diversity which helps to make the country attractive to investors.

Page 31–33 · Reading 3

In general terms, the word ‘capital’ is used to refer to money, but it also has a more specific meaning. Capital refers to any item which is produced for the purpose of assisting in the production of other goods and services. Cranes, servers, office buildings, ships, and lorries, for example, are all examples of capital goods. Investment in capital goods helps countries to produce more goods and services in the future.

A multinational company is a company that has its headquarters in one country but may have many other departments, offices, outlets or factories in other countries. Multinationals are also referred to as Trans-National Corporations (TNCs). […] With this investment they create jobs. […] Companies operating in different countries may also have to pay local taxes, which can help boost government revenues which can be used to provide public services.

Checkpoint: Do multinationals always bring benefits to a country like the UAE? Explain.

Page 33–35 · Reading 4

Greater awareness. It is often said that globalisation has led to the world ‘shrinking’. What is meant by this is that the improved connectedness between countries means that it is more likely that events and issues in one part of the world will not go unnoticed in other parts of the world. […] As more people are made aware of disasters like earthquakes, help, assistance and support can be mobilised much quicker to help those affected.

Increases in inequality. Globalisation tends to favour companies who are very large and who have considerable power and influence both economically and politically. […] The result can be that the rich countries get richer and the poor countries get poorer.

No ‘trickle-down’ effect. One of the arguments for globalisation is that the investment in countries that is made by companies as they expand their operations will lead to what is called a ‘trickle-down’ effect. The wealth that is created will flow down to all parts of the community in a country and make everyone better off. […] There are many people who question whether there is any evidence to support this view.

Page 36–38 · Reading 5

The effect on local businesses. […] Smaller businesses may not be able to compete on price or quality and can be forced out of business. This can lead to a reduction in local services as well as unemployment for those who were involved in the business.

Cultural diversity. […] One of the arguments against globalisation is that it can threaten culture and tradition. […] The UAE is trying to diversify its economy so that it does not rely on one or two key products for its future wealth and prosperity.

Working conditions and health and safety. […] A criticism of globalisation is that companies can avoid these costs by locating operations in countries which do not have such laws and protection. […] at the risk of greater exploitation of workers.

“We must not rely on oil alone as the main source of our national income. We have to diversify the sources of our revenue […].” Sheikh Zayed bin Sultan Al Nahyan (May Allah have mercy upon his soul)

Page 38–42 · Reading 6

Fresh water is scarce in the UAE, so conservation and secure supplies are important. The textbook describes Liwa’s underground water reserve as a water-security example. Avoid repeating an undated “top ten” ranking without its measure and source.

Estimate tap flow without wasting drinking water. Use a published flow rating or measure water already needed for a normal task, with adult supervision. If you collect water, use a small safe container and reuse it appropriately. Do not leave a tap running for two minutes just for this activity.
