CONCEPT CARD

Ethical consumer choices

Consumers can be individuals, businesses (intermediate consumers) or governments. Choices raise ethical questions. The clothing industry gives jobs and low prices, but workers can face poor conditions (as at Rana Plaza) and clothing creates waste. We can reflect on our own choices.

Our buying choices have effects on people and the environment.

Words to know

ethicsideas about what is right and wrong (from Grade 9)profit marginthe difference between the selling price and the cost of production
consumersomeone who buys goods and servicesfast fashioncheap clothes that people replace often
factor inputsthe things a business needs to operate, such as labour and raw materialsbiodegradeto break down naturally (polyester does not)
intermediate consumera business that buys something as part of its production processcarbon footprintthe amount of carbon dioxide caused by an activity

① Name three types of consumer. ② Name a benefit and a cost of fashion. ③ How might my choices change?

Page 73–75 · Reading 1

What factors do you consider when you want to buy something? Are the choices you make as a customer based on ethics?

You are an example of a consumer because you buy goods and services. However, you are not the only type of consumer. […] Individuals as consumers: We might typically think of consumers as being people like you and your classmates and teachers who buy goods and services every day. […]

Businesses as consumers: Businesses need labour and have to buy many different types of raw materials, machinery, equipment, buildings, computers, and stock to help with the production process. These are all called ‘factor inputs’. […] Businesses, are therefore, consumers as well but they tend to be what are called ‘intermediate consumers’ in that what they are buying is not for final consumption […] but as part of their production process.

Governments as consumers: Governments around the world are major buyers of many different types of goods and services. […] They also provide services such as health, education, justice and defence.

Page 76–78 · Reading 2

Checkpoint: Do you remember from your work in Grade 9 what the word ‘ethics’ means? […] We are going to present a series of situations which involve ethical considerations and ask you to think about what the ethical issues might be.

Scenario 1. A small team of senior executives from a US IT company meet with executives from a company based in the UAE. The US team are hoping to sell their software which helps manage accounting systems. The deal could be worth several million dollars to the US company. […] Before dinner, the US team present each of the UAE executive team with an expensive branded watch as ‘Thanks for considering their product’.

Scenario 2. A company in the UAE selling carpets and rugs for houses has been having a difficult time. […] The managing director of the company becomes aware of a supplier from Afghanistan who can supply similar high quality carpets but at very much lower prices. […] the managing director learns that one of the reasons the carpets are priced so low is that the supplier uses child labour and pays them less than a dollar a day. […] If he continues with the high-priced carpets from the UK, the chances are he will have to close the business, but if he can sell carpets at a much lower price, the business will be able to survive.

Page 79–81 · Reading 3

Scenario 3. Around 80% of the UAE is desert. The towns and cities of the UAE are growing rapidly, however, and with the growth in population comes the problem of waste. […] The government asks local businesses and universities to propose a solution to the waste issue. Two options are proposed: Option 1 utilises the vast space in the desert to build more landfill space […]. Option 2 is to build three waste incineration plants in different locations of the UAE, which will take in 60% of all waste produced in the UAE. The incineration of the waste will generate energy which can be used to power homes and businesses across the UAE.

Discussion Point: When you have completed your responses to the three scenarios, read some of the responses from your classmates. Discuss what differences there are in responses and why there might be differences in the way people in your class have responded to these scenarios.

It can be seen from the scenarios above that making purchasing decisions is not easy and can raise a number of ethical issues. You may not be in a position to influence business or government purchasing decisions directly, but your actions can have an effect on businesses and the way in which they carry out their operations.

Page 82–84 · Reading 4

On 24 April 2013, the Rana Plaza building collapsed in Savar, near Dhaka, Bangladesh. The building was used as a factory making clothing for the fashion industry. Inside the building at the time of the collapse were around 3100 workers. Of these, over 1100 died and many of the survivors were reported to be injured. […] The day before the collapse, it was reported that there were cracks in the building, and it was evacuated whilst checks were carried out. The owner of the building declared that it was safe for workers to return. The following day, the building collapsed.

The factories in the Rana Plaza building produced clothes for leading global brands. […] Following the disaster, many other factories were checked and a number were closed due to safety concerns. The closures resulted in many people losing their jobs. In 2016, research carried out by the Asia Floor Wage Alliance suggested that despite the disaster, conditions for workers in clothing factories across Bangladesh had not improved dramatically.

Discussion Point: Given the information about the Rana Plaza disaster, discuss whether it is right to buy clothes from Bangladesh where worker health and safety is still an issue.

Page 85–87 · Reading 5

The textbook quotes historical market-size, employment and profit-margin estimates from the 2010s. They illustrate the size of the clothing industry, not its current values. Global trade can create jobs and lower prices, while also shifting jobs and exposing workers to risks. Compare the benefits with labour standards, safety and environmental costs.

Thinking Task: Why do you think the profit margins in the clothing industry are relatively low?

Clothing production uses energy, water, land and chemicals and produces waste. Environmental estimates vary by date, boundary and method. The textbook’s “10% footprint” and aviation comparison are not a verified current like-for-like measure. Discuss the mechanisms rather than presenting those percentages as settled current facts.

Page 88–91 · Reading 6

Action Task: Think about going shopping in a mall for some new clothes. What are the key things you are thinking about as you browse to make your choice? You see some tee-shirts in the store which are priced at 20 AED. What is the first thing that you think about when seeing this price? You look at the label in a garment and see that it was ‘Made in Bangladesh’. What are your immediate thoughts? […] Much of the discussion in this lesson has been about clothing but what issues might there be with regard to the ethics of buying food?

In November 2017, shoppers at a well-known clothes store in Istanbul were surprised when they found labels inside some garments in the store saying “I made this item you are going to buy, but I didn’t get paid for it”. […] If you had come across these labels, what would your thoughts and actions be?

Self-Assessment Task: What do you think is meant by ‘ethical decision making’ in consumption? Is ethical decision making confined to individuals only? Explain. […] Write a reflective piece on whether your consumption decisions in the future will change. If they do, in what way will they change and why? If not, why not?
