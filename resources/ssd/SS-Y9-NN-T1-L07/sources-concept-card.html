📖 Book text

Student Book Vol. 1 · Original text from the Student Book.

Page 76–77 · Reading 1

How is wealth measured? What happened during the 2007–2008 financial crisis? Activity 1: Read “The Moral Value of Money”. The book says wealth can bring many good things: people can meet their basic needs of food, shelter and clothing, then take care of their wants. Wealth also benefits others, because people with wealth are better able to donate to charity, invest in businesses and promote their community. However, money is neither good nor bad; it is morally neutral, and how you use it gives it its moral value.

Task: Think about how money could lead to good or bad deeds. Identify at least four good and four bad deeds that money could cause.

Page 78–79 · Reading 2

Activity 2: Read about the Human Development Index. The book asks why we strive so hard to make money, and says money is simply a means to an end. It says people in the past waged wars to increase their resources, possessions and access to clean water. Wealth is all our valuable possessions, including money, and the book says it has been historically associated with negative ideas such as greed and inequality. It says that if a nation is wealthy, it should not have high inequalities between incomes, and that inequality may be due to corruption and greed.

UNDP’s Human Development Index combines three dimensions: a long and healthy life, knowledge, and a decent standard of living. The indicators are life expectancy at birth; mean and expected years of schooling; and gross national income per person. The index ranges from 0 to 1. The table in this textbook is historical, showing the UAE in the very-high-development group for 2015. HDI does not by itself measure inequality or every aspect of wellbeing.

Questions: (1) Why do you think the UAE has such a high HDI? (2) How would you recommend countries with lower HDI indices approach their problems? (3) Does having more money mean you are more developed as a person?

Page 79–81 · Reading 3

Activity 3: Read about the 2007–2008 financial crisis. A combination of a housing boom, risky lending, complex financial products, weak risk management and other factors made the system vulnerable. The crisis was not caused by every borrower behaving in the same way.

The 2007–2008 financial crisis followed a housing and mortgage-credit boom. Subprime mortgages were higher-risk loans, not loans defined simply by the absence of a repayment guarantee. Falling home prices and missed payments increased losses on mortgage-related securities and weakened financial institutions. Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy in September 2008. The crisis contributed to the Great Recession, not a second Great Depression; the US recession lasted from December 2007 to June 2009, and effects spread internationally.

Task: With your group, make a presentation on one question: (a) What is the problem addressed in the article? (b) What are the moral problems highlighted by the crisis? (c) What lessons can we learn from the crisis?

Page 81–82 · Reading 4

Activity 4: Read the text about corruption in sport. The book says sports can promote values such as fair play, team spirit and hard work, but the arena can also lead to corruption. It says that in recent years sports have been tainted by scandals, with many officials charged with taking bribes in return for favours. The book says corruption also spread into marketing and sponsorship, where there are vast sums of money, and that bribes were used to secure media and marketing rights to tournaments. It says some high-profile individuals across the world faced jail time, and reminds us that the principles that drive most sports people are fair play, hard work, team effort and support for all players.

In pairs, discuss: (1) What do you think of what you have just read? Are you surprised or disappointed? Explain why. (2) What effect does this kind of financial corruption scandal have on sport? (3) What could be done to stop this happening in the future? (4) What was the consequence of the corruption?

Page 83–85 · Reading 5

Activity 5: Read the article about financial literacy. The book says that following the global financial crisis the Emirates Foundation–Esref Sah Programme was set up to teach the UAE’s young people financial literacy and money management. It says debt levels have risen and that debt itself is not necessarily bad; too much debt is the problem. The book says over 40,000 people aged 15 to 35 have received financial education, and that of those who attended workshops, 64% started saving, 26% started looking for investment opportunities and 80% became more aware of financial literacy.

The book says a volunteer programme has trained 100 volunteers, that bank staff were trained on how to market products to young Emiratis, and that in 2016 the Abu Dhabi Commercial Bank announced a collaboration with the Emirates Foundation to raise financial literacy. Questions: (1) Is teaching young people to manage money a good thing? Why? (2) What are the dangers of financial illiteracy? (3) What can you do to manage your money better? (4) What role do banks have to play?

Activity 6: The book gives a statement by Sheikh Zayed bin Sultan Al Nahyan that buildings, schools, hospitals and bridges are material things, and that the real spirit behind progress is the human spirit. With your group, create a poster that says what it means to you, how it relates to the lesson, and decorate it. Present it to the class.
