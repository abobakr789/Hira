CONCEPT CARD

Crises of the Late Middle Ages

The Great Famine of 1315 to 1317 CE followed a cooling climate, heavy rain and floods, and food prices rose. The Black Death (bubonic plague) was caused by bacteria carried on fleas and spread quickly where hygiene was poor, from 1347 to 1351. The book gives estimates of how many died. Living standards generally improved afterwards.

Health protects communities.

What is a natural disaster? Why is health and sanitation important for public well-being? What are the effects of natural disasters on people and the planet?

Activity 1: The pictures show some natural disasters: a volcano, an earthquake and a drought. What happens to people and the planet during these times?

Page 156 · Reading 2

Wars and battles for territory were not the only problem that Europeans faced during the Late Middle Ages. Several other natural disasters also occurred during this time that had terrible consequences all over Europe. One of these disasters was the Great Famine of 1315 to 1317 CE. […] By the end of the 13th century CE, the weather was getting cooler and cooler. Summers were getting shorter and winters were getting longer and colder. […] Later historians would call this period of time the Little Ice Age, and it would lead to one of the worst famines in European history. The spring of 1315 CE was hit by very heavy rain across all of Europe, causing large floods and damaging the grains that had been stored over the winter. […] food prices doubled and then tripled.

Page 157 · Reading 3

In France, wheat became too expensive to use for baking bread, and many poor people had nothing to eat. The famine continued for the next two years, with millions of people starving to death. Some historians estimate that the Great Famine killed up to 25% of the population of Europe. Another major crisis to hit Europe was the bubonic plague, which was called the Black Death. […] The bubonic plague was caused by bacteria carried on fleas which lived on rodents. […] In crowded European cities, where personal hygiene practices were poor, the bubonic plague spread quickly. Some scientists estimate that the plague killed up to 200 million people, a third to half of all Europeans, in just four years, from 1347 to 1351. […] Living standards generally improved as wages increased and the price of food and other goods fell. Estimate check: figures vary by region and source. Smith College gives 5–12% for northern Europe in the famine; Britannica estimates about 25 million European Black Death deaths. Do not equate 200 million with one-third of Europe.

Activity 5: From the beginning of the 14th century until the middle of the 19th century, Europe experienced the Little Ice Age, a time when temperatures were especially cool. What were the reasons for the change in climate? To what extent did human activity contribute? Was the Black Death a factor? Do some research and answer the questions. Share your findings with the class.
