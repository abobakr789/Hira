📖 Book text

Student Book Vol. 1 · Original text from the Student Book.

Page 145–147 · Reading 1

Activity 1: Read about falaj irrigation. Hili in Al Ain preserves one of the oldest known examples of aflaj, dating to the Iron Age. These channels carried water to farms and settlements. Archaeological evidence supports their early importance in this region; avoid turning “one of the oldest known” into an uncontested claim of a single worldwide inventor.

Around 1,000 BCE, the book says, rainfall dropped slightly again and water became even harder to reach. At the coastal town of Muweilah in the Sharjah region, people used new types of wells that tapped the fresh water lying above salty water, but even these sources, and the inland springs, would eventually have been affected. People needed water from deeper underground and a system to carry it to the surface.

Page 147–148 · Reading 2

The book says the falaj system was the solution. A falaj is a water-channel system carrying water to where it is needed. Aflaj can include underground tunnels and open channels, fed by groundwater, springs or surface water. It says the falaj had a big impact on human life: around 1,000 BCE new villages and towns grew in the UAE interior, especially around the Al Ain area and on the edges of the Al Hajar Mountains. Archaeologists discovered many buildings at sites like Hili, probably all part of a single ancient town. Towns in the interior like Hili were possible because of the falaj system, which also provided water for many of the oases that developed. The book says the falaj system remained one of the most remarkable features of agriculture and village life in the UAE, and its impact lasts to the present day.

Questions: What did people in Sharjah do to get fresh water? Why were wells and springs not enough? How did having aflaj affect human life around 1000 BCE? How are the aflaj and oases related? How do you think the falaj being underground saved water?

Page 148–149 · Reading 3

Activity 2: Can you think of things made of iron? Can you think of things made from steel, which comes from iron? Maybe look them up on the Internet or other sources to find out. How common are these items in our world today? The book gives a table headed “Things Made from Iron”. Activity 4: Do a search on the Internet to find at least 3 modern-day items made from copper, bronze, iron, and steel.

Page 150–152 · Reading 4

Activity 3: Read “The Iron Age”. The book says the Iron Age was the third stage in the “three-age” period: Stone Age (up to about 3,000 BCE), Bronze Age (about 3,000–1,200 BCE) and Iron Age (about 1,200–100 BCE), and that the Iron Age was marked by the discovery and use of iron. It says the Iron Age was recognised at different times in different parts of the world, because iron and the technologies to use it appeared at different times. In some parts of the world iron replaced bronze for tools and weapons because iron was more durable; in other parts, like the UAE area, people continued to use bronze more than iron because they had easier access to copper and the technology to convert it into bronze.

The book says that around 1,000 BCE houses in the UAE area were made more and more of mudbrick, which relied on knowing which materials to use and how to design buildings that would last. Some buildings were still standing when archaeologists found them 3,000 years later, and the mudbrick houses of al-Ain, Fujairah, Dubai or Sharjah 100 years ago are in many ways the result of this early mastery.

Page 151–154 · Reading 5

The book says food in the Iron Age was mostly the same as before: people ate sheep, goats and cattle, hunted wild animals like gazelles and oryx, got milk from domesticated animals, and continued to catch fish and collect shellfish along the coast. The use of the falaj changed the crops that could be grown: there is evidence that sesame was cultivated, for oil or flour, and the herb basil and other plants were grown in small gardens made possible by the falaj.

Questions: What are the approximate times for each age in the UAE area? Why did people prefer iron instead of bronze? Why didn’t the Iron Age start at the same time all over the world? What material did people start to use to make houses? Why can we say that this material is very durable? What crops could people grow after the falaj?

Activity 5: The Al Ain Oasis was designated by UNESCO (the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization) as a World Heritage Site. Make a list of reasons why it is important that we recognise and preserve historical sites like the Al Ain Oasis. Activity 6: Take your ideas and write them as a paragraph saying why we need to preserve world heritage sites like the Al Ain Oasis.
