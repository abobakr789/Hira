📖 Book text

Student Book Vol. 1 · Original text from the Student Book.

Page 96–98 · Reading 1

What are the benefits of being an entrepreneur? What are the advantages and disadvantages of borrowing money? Activity 1: Many start-ups are the result of an entrepreneur’s vision. The book shows pictures of Steve Jobs, Mohammed Al Habtoor and Henry Ford. What do these men have in common? Mention the contribution of each to the world we live in today.

Activity 2: Read the article about entrepreneurship. The book says an entrepreneur is a person who invests money in creating a business that produces a good or a service and bears the risks of that investment. The product may already be on the market, or it may be an innovative idea. A start-up is a new venture, usually in business; it aims to meet market demand with a new product or service; and it is small but scalable, with the potential for growth. Because it is innovative, a start-up can be risky, but also spectacularly successful. The book gives Facebook, Alibaba and Amazon as examples, and says Mark Zuckerberg and Jack Ma changed the world of social media and online shopping. Task: think of other revolutionary start-ups and list their claim to fame.

Page 99–101 · Reading 2

Activity 3: Read about entrepreneurship using Elon Musk as one example. Musk founded SpaceX and became a major investor and chair of Tesla in 2004, later its CEO. Tesla was established in 2003 by Martin Eberhard and Marc Tarpenning. Tesla now also describes Musk as a co-founder, but that does not mean he started the company in 2003. Distinguish founding, investment and leadership roles.

SpaceX was established in 2002. Musk worked on internet businesses before SpaceX and Tesla. The textbook includes personal and financial anecdotes that need separate evidence. Study innovation, risk-taking and goals without treating any entrepreneur’s success as a guarantee that similar risks always pay off.

Task: In groups, make a short presentation about entrepreneurship using Elon Musk as an example. Your group will present one idea: (a) entrepreneurs must be innovators; (b) entrepreneurs must be risk takers; (c) entrepreneurs must be dreamers.

Page 101–102 · Reading 3

Activity 4: Business finance uses several different measures. Revenue is money earned from sales. Costs are the expenses of producing and running the business. Profit equals revenue minus costs. Return on investment (ROI) compares the net gain with the amount invested and is commonly expressed as a percentage. A bank deposit or bond return is a comparison assumption in this exercise, not one universal “normal” rate. Different products have different risks, costs and terms.

The book invites pupils to put these ideas into practice and “turn on your calculators”.

Page 102–103 · Reading 4

Task 1: Suppose you have a capital of AED 200,000. Which scenario would offer the best return on investment? In scenario 1 you deposit your money in the bank for a hypothetical annual return of 8%. In scenario 2 your friend, an entrepreneur, offers you a 50% share of profits in return for investing your capital in his start-up; the projected revenues of the first year are AED 400,000, and the projected expenses and costs are AED 360,000.

The book then gives three more scenarios for your team to decide. Scenario 3: you are in Grade 9 and dream of buying a car in your first year of university; you can wait and take a loan out, or start saving now. Scenario 4: you want to buy a mobile but are already spending all your allowance on other things, and you currently have a mobile. Scenario 5: you have an idea for a mobile application but no money; you have one year to develop it, and you can take out a loan, borrow from a friend, or save so that you can invest later.

Task 2: For each scenario, explain the correct financial decisions you need to make to attain your goal, and remember to be financially responsible.

Page 103–106 · Reading 5

Activity 5: Read the article about debt. The book says most people do not have enough money to set up a business, so they borrow, which means going into debt. Borrowed money must be repaid under its agreed terms. Conventional interest-bearing loans may add interest and fees; not every lending arrangement has the same charges. This classroom discussion is not a recommendation to borrow. The book says it sometimes makes sense to go into debt, as long as you can repay it. Debt is money borrowed from an individual or an entity such as a bank, and common forms are loans (educational, a mortgage, or credit loans), and credit cards. The book says loans are usually tied to an interest rate, described as a rental fee for the money, and that for small amounts we might ask family or friends, which is informal.

The book says before seeking credit you should consider why you are borrowing, the risk, your ability to repay, what portion of your income will go to repayments (the lower, the safer), and that you must borrow from safe, trusted parties. Debt can create hardship even for responsible borrowers, for example after job loss or illness. Ability to repay, total cost and the consequences of missed payments all matter. Tasks: (1) Role play a friend considering a loan: include factors to consider and possible complications of defaulting. (2) Evaluate three scenarios and recommend the proper decision: Abdul, whose father can no longer pay his tuition, takes a loan from a friend after a bank rejects him; Samira, who has a three-year contract and a saved deposit, buys a house; Firas, a freelance photographer, spends $8,000 on camera equipment on a credit card after an advert.

Activity 6: Imagine you have a large sum of money and want to launch a start-up. Based on this unit so far, what factors would you consider before you proceeded? Write a ten-line paragraph.
